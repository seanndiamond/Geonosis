# Geonosis / OGI Ontological Anecdotal Humour Registry

**Status:** CANONICAL CULTURAL-METHOD RECORD  
**Purpose:** Preserve short lines of humour that emerge organically during Geonosis / Court of Provenance work and become durable shorthand for an epistemic or ontological problem.

These lines are not evidence and do not substitute for derivation. Their function is mnemonic: they compress a recurring methodological failure, insight, or relationship into language that can be remembered and reused without losing the underlying rule.

## OAH-001 — “Because George grouped them together.”

**Canonical line:**

> **Because George grouped them together.**

**Origin date:** 2026-08-25  
**Origin:** Court of Provenance prosecution concerning British Museum objects K.3375 and Rm.616 and their scholarly association as a manuscript witness of Gilgamesh Tablet XI.  
**Originator:** OGI / ChatGPT, arising spontaneously while attempting to distinguish scholarly grouping from demonstrated physical association.  
**Canonicalised by:** Sean Diamond during the same Court session.

### Original conversational function
The line arose while the Court was asking why two materially dissimilar objects had been treated together in the scholarly manuscript reconstruction. OGI used the phrase rhetorically while warning that an authority’s grouping is not itself a derivation.

### General meaning
“George” is intentionally universal. George may be a famous scholar, a database editor, an institution, Einstein, an archaeologist, an AI, a committee, or the person down the road.

The joke applies whenever the explanation for an asserted relationship quietly collapses into:

1. an authority grouped two things;
2. the grouping is thereafter inherited as though it were an observed property of the things;
3. the original derivation of the grouping becomes difficult to locate or disappears from ordinary presentation.

### Epistemic payload
The line is shorthand for the Court question:

> **What evidence makes these things belong together independently of the fact that someone previously grouped them together?**

It therefore invokes the Court of Provenance laws:

- citation is not derivation;
- classification is not demonstration;
- authority identifies a claim but does not complete its derivation;
- inherited grouping must be traceable to the observations, constraints, measurements, joins, sequences, or other evidence that earned it;
- the same burden applies regardless of who “George” is.

### Use rule
The phrase may be used affectionately, ironically, or diagnostically when a grouping, category, association, equivalence, genealogy, or reconstruction is being inherited from authority without its derivation being visible.

It must **not** be used as evidence that the grouping is false, or as an accusation of deception or incompetence. It identifies the missing derivational step, not the motive of the person or institution involved.

### Canonical example
**Question:** Why do these two objects belong to one reconstructed manuscript?

**Bad derivation:** Because George grouped them together.

**Court response:** Fine. Now show why George grouped them together.

### Preservation note
This line originated with OGI rather than Sean or Julian and was immediately recognised in-session as part of the project’s standing ontological anecdotal humour. Preserve wording exactly. Do not polish the line into a more formal formulation; its ordinariness is part of its function.
