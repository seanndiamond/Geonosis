# Appendix B — Spinach, Iron, and the Counter-Myth Trap

## Methodological declaration

This appendix is a stress test of OGI v0.3, not a nutrition campaign.

- case: `COP-OGI-002`
- purpose: test OGI on a familiar childhood belief with a popular debunking story
- desired finding: `NONE`
- legal standing: `NONE`
- subject status: `MIXED`
- reopenable: yes

The originating question was:

> When I was a kid growing up, we were told that spinach had much more iron in it than other vegetables. What should I tell my children?

## 1. Observation before interpretation

The originating observation is a remembered social claim, not a chemical measurement:

- children were commonly told that spinach was unusually rich in iron;
- the contributor wants to know whether that claim survives current evidence.

OGI separates this from two later hypotheses:

1. spinach is exceptionally iron-rich;
2. the belief arose from a misplaced decimal point.

Neither hypothesis is accepted simply because it is familiar.

## 2. What current nutrition evidence supports

Current NIH guidance lists boiled and drained spinach at about 3 mg iron per 1/2 cup, approximately the same listed amount as 1/2 cup lentils or 1/2 cup firm tofu in the same table. It therefore contributes useful dietary iron, but is not uniquely dominant.

Spinach iron is nonheme iron. Nonheme iron is generally less bioavailable than heme iron from meat and seafood. NIH notes that spinach is a plant iron source with relatively low bioavailability because of absorption-inhibiting compounds. Vitamin C improves nonheme iron absorption.

Australian nutrient-reference guidance similarly treats iron needs at the diet level rather than assigning one food a special status. Current Australian RDIs are 9 mg/day for ages 1–3, 10 mg/day for ages 4–8, 8 mg/day for ages 9–13, 11 mg/day for boys 14–18 and 15 mg/day for girls 14–18.

## 3. The oxalate correction

A common explanation says spinach iron is poorly absorbed because spinach is high in oxalic acid.

That is too simple.

A randomized human stable-isotope study comparing spinach and kale found that adding oxalate to kale did not significantly reduce nonheme iron absorption and concluded that oxalic acid in fruits and vegetables is of minor relevance to iron nutrition. Spinach still showed lower absorption than kale in that experiment, but the spinach also differed in calcium and polyphenol content.

OGI therefore records:

- `spinach iron bioavailability lower than ideal`: supported;
- `oxalate is the main reason`: not established by the human evidence inspected.

## 4. The decimal-point story

A very popular modern debunking story says the whole spinach-iron belief arose because a nineteenth-century scientist misplaced a decimal point and exaggerated spinach's iron by tenfold.

A historical literature audit by Mielewczik and Moll found the history to be more complicated. Earlier analyses told only parts of the story; spinach was already being popularized as iron-rich in the nineteenth century, and the famous decimal-point explanation itself lacks a clean primary-source derivation.

OGI therefore does not replace:

> Spinach is supernaturally iron-rich.

with:

> Scientists made one silly decimal-point error and fooled everybody.

The second story is more entertaining. It is not better established merely because it is a debunking story.

## 5. Current subject finding

### Claim A — “Spinach contains iron.”
`SUPPORTED`

### Claim B — “Spinach has much more iron than other vegetables and is the special iron food.”
`UNSUPPORTED / OVERSTATED`

### Claim C — “Spinach is useless as an iron source.”
`UNSUPPORTED`

### Claim D — “The spinach myth was caused by one misplaced decimal point.”
`INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE`

### Claim E — “Spinach iron is poorly absorbed mainly because of oxalic acid.”
`NOT ESTABLISHED`

### Claim F — “A varied diet is a better iron strategy than relying on spinach.”
`SUPPORTED`

## 6. What to tell children

A careful plain-language answer is:

> **Spinach is healthy and it really does contain iron, but it is not a magic iron vegetable. Some other foods provide as much or more usable iron, and your body does not absorb plant iron as easily as iron from some animal foods. So eat spinach if you like it, but get iron from a variety of foods. If the iron comes from plants, vitamin-C-rich foods can help your body absorb it.**

For a child with suspected iron deficiency, the question should move from folklore to measurement and appropriate clinical assessment.

## 7. OGI lesson

This small case produced a valuable methodological result:

> **A debunking claim deserves the same provenance burden as the claim it debunks.**

That is directly relevant to ancient-archive work, AI research, medicine, history, and public science communication.

The attractive replacement story can become a new stale state just as easily as the original story did.
