## Current subject state

The first run supports that:

- spinach contains a meaningful amount of iron;
- boiled spinach provides about 3 mg iron per 1/2 cup in current NIH/USDA-derived nutrition tables;
- spinach is not uniquely or magically iron-rich compared with other useful iron foods such as lentils and tofu;
- the iron in spinach is nonheme iron and is less bioavailable than heme iron from animal foods;
- spinach contains compounds that can reduce nonheme iron availability, although oxalic acid itself appears to be of minor relevance to iron absorption in human isotope studies;
- vitamin C can improve absorption of nonheme iron;
- the popular story that spinach's iron reputation was caused by a single misplaced decimal point is itself not securely established and should not be repeated as fact.

The first run does **not** support telling children that spinach is useless for iron.

## Plain-language answer

Tell children:

> Spinach is a healthy food and it does contain iron, but it is not a magic iron food. Your body does not absorb all of its iron very easily, so iron is better thought of as something you get from a varied diet. Beans, lentils, tofu, fortified cereals, meat and seafood can all contribute. If your meal uses plant iron, eating vitamin-C-rich foods with it can help absorption.

If iron deficiency is suspected, spinach is not a treatment substitute. Assessment should be based on appropriate medical evaluation rather than food folklore.

## Why this case matters to OGI

This case revealed a classic epistemic trap:

1. inherited claim: `spinach is extraordinarily rich in iron`;
2. debunking claim: `that belief came from one decimal-point error`;
3. deeper audit: the first claim is overstated, while the second is also poorly evidenced.

OGI therefore has to preserve a less theatrical answer:

> Spinach is a legitimate but not exceptional iron source, and the famous decimal-point origin story is itself an academic legend with an incomplete derivation chain.
