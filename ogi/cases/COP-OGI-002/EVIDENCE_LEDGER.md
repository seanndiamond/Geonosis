# COP-OGI-002 Evidence Ledger

## E-001 — Spinach contains dietary iron

**Source:** NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Iron Fact Sheet for Health Professionals, current table derived from USDA FoodData Central.

**Observation:** 1/2 cup boiled and drained spinach is listed at about 3 mg iron.

**Claim supported:** `spinach contains a meaningful amount of dietary iron`

**Court state:** `SUPPORTED`

**Boundary:** Does not establish exceptional status or high absorption.

---

## E-002 — Spinach is not uniquely dominant among useful iron foods

**Source:** Same NIH table.

**Observation:** 1/2 cup boiled lentils and 1/2 cup firm tofu are also listed at about 3 mg iron; white beans and fortified cereals can provide more per listed serving.

**Claim supported:** `spinach is one useful iron-containing food among several`

**Court state:** `SUPPORTED`

**Boundary:** Serving sizes, preparation and food density differ; this is not a universal ranking of all foods.

---

## E-003 — Spinach iron has lower bioavailability than heme iron

**Source:** NIH iron guidance.

**Observation:** Heme iron is generally more bioavailable than nonheme iron. NIH specifically notes spinach as a plant iron source with low bioavailability because of iron-absorption inhibitors.

**Claim supported:** `total spinach iron is not equivalent to absorbed iron`

**Court state:** `SUPPORTED`

**Boundary:** Does not mean spinach contributes no absorbed iron.

---

## E-004 — Vitamin C improves nonheme iron absorption

**Source:** NIH iron guidance.

**Observation:** Ascorbic acid enhances nonheme iron bioavailability.

**Claim supported:** `pairing plant iron foods with vitamin-C-rich foods can improve absorption`

**Court state:** `SUPPORTED`

---

## E-005 — Oxalic acid is not established as the main explanation for low spinach iron absorption

**Source:** Storcksdieck genannt Bonsmann et al., European Journal of Clinical Nutrition 62 (2008), randomized crossover stable-isotope studies.

**Observation:** Added oxalate to kale did not significantly reduce nonheme iron absorption. Authors concluded oxalic acid in fruits and vegetables is of minor relevance to iron nutrition. Spinach absorption was lower than kale in the comparison, but spinach also differed in calcium and polyphenols.

**Claim supported:** `simple oxalate-blocks-iron explanation is insufficient`

**Court state:** `SUPPORTED`

**Boundary:** Does not establish which specific spinach compounds account for all observed bioavailability differences.

---

## E-006 — Historical misplaced-decimal explanation is not securely derived

**Source:** Mielewczik & Moll (2019), *Spinach in Blunderland: How the myth that spinach is rich in iron became an urban academic legend*; academic discussion of the Hamblin citation chain.

**Observation:** Historical scholarship finds that spinach was promoted as iron-rich from the nineteenth century, while the famous misplaced-decimal account itself lacks a clean traceable primary-source foundation and became repeatedly copied through later literature.

**Claim supported:** `the decimal-point origin story should not be stated as settled fact`

**Court state:** `SUPPORTED`

**Boundary:** Does not prove that no transcription or arithmetic error ever occurred in any historical spinach analysis.

---

## E-007 — Australian child iron requirements are age dependent

**Source:** Australian Eat for Health / NHMRC Nutrient Reference Values.

**Observation:** RDIs vary by age and sex, including 9 mg/day ages 1–3, 10 mg/day ages 4–8, 8 mg/day ages 9–13, 11 mg/day boys 14–18, and 15 mg/day girls 14–18.

**Claim supported:** `iron adequacy is a whole-diet and life-stage question`

**Court state:** `SUPPORTED`

**Boundary:** Population reference values do not diagnose an individual child.
