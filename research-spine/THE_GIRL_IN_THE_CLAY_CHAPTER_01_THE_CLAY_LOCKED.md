# THE GIRL IN THE CLAY
## Chapter One: The Clay
**Status: EDITORIAL LOCK — 21 November 2026**
**Continuity note:** Susan Elliott (double t); professional archive-reading method routine in Veyra. The rarity is an apparent sustained biography of one individual. Do not foreshadow later Veyra events.

Seawater speckled the helicopter windscreen, just enough for the automatic wipers to make two passes.

Morning sunlight pierced the retreating storm clouds, scattering pink and orange across an ocean still arguing with the weather.

Jim Climham enjoyed these journeys home.

Seven weeks at an excavation had left him with aching knees, a collection of scratches and an unreasonable longing for a proper shower. He loved the field. He loved the patience of it, the quiet moment when a deposit began to make sense, the conversations with people who knew the land better than he ever would.

But he also loved coming home.

He was particularly looking forward to seeing Susan Elliott's deep brown eyes, and to being on the receiving end of that warm, entirely personal smile of hers.

His neighbour Ellie would be less enthusiastic. After his previous expedition, she had lodged a complaint about the volume of his singing in the shower.

Jim had considered her position carefully.

He intended to sing anyway.

The helicopter banked towards Veyra's harbour.

Far below, a red trawler rose along the back of a tremendous wave, tipped over the crest and slid down the other side. Beyond it lay the cultivated seaweed beds and crustacean farms, their long ordered shapes following the curve of the coast.

The sea was generous to the city.

It was not, however, obedient.

The helicopter turned inland.

Veyra opened before him.

Glass towers climbed above the waterfront, their curved faces catching the new sunlight. Elevated gardens joined the buildings, carrying trees and flowering vines above the busy streets. A silver train crossed the river and vanished between two green terraces.

Small aircraft moved among the towers. Below, people filled the market squares and promenades. Children travelled with their families. Animals moved among them, some familiar, some rather less so.

Near a market entrance, an elephant stood beside a sleeping man who had chosen an inconvenient patch of roadway for his nap. The handler gestured for the elephant to move him.

The elephant declined.

A second handler arrived. All three appeared to consider the matter.

Jim smiled.

There were advantages to being an elephant. Few people expected you to hurry.

The helicopter settled on the institute's upper landing platform.

Jim collected his duffel and the smaller protective case within it. Before leaving the cabin, he checked the case clasp.

Still secure.

He had checked it six times already.

He checked it once more.

***

Susan Elliott was comparing a series of geological images when Jim's arrival notice appeared beside her workstation.

She read it and smiled.

He hadn't mentioned that he was coming home today.

That generally meant he had something worth showing her.

In the adjoining laboratory, Mark Whaler was examining a mineral specimen beneath a suspended light. He had the absent-minded expression of a man who had spent the morning in a disagreement with a machine.

"Jim's back," Susan said.

Mark looked up.

"With anything interesting?"

"He hasn't told me."

"Then definitely."

Susan laughed.

"Come on."

Jim was in reception when they found him, sun-weathered, dusty at the knees and looking far too pleased with himself.

"Susan."

She crossed the hall and embraced him.

"Welcome home."

"Lovely to be back."

She touched the air near the scratch on his cheek.

"What happened there?"

"A rock."

"Who won?"

"The rock."

Mark shook Jim's hand.

"How was the expedition?"

"Excellent."

"The weather?"

"Terrible."

"The accommodation?"

"Worse."

"A triumph, then."

"By archaeological standards."

Susan glanced at the duffel.

"What's in the bag?"

Jim tried to look innocent.

After twenty years of friendship, the attempt was hopeless.

"Something for your table."

"How big?"

"Three centimetres."

Mark smiled.

"Does it have a provenance?"

"Of course it has a provenance."

"Then we may as well get to work."

***

Jim laid out the field photographs first.

Susan reviewed the location, depth, surrounding deposit and associated fragments while Mark prepared the imaging table. They worked easily, interrupting one another only when something needed clarification.

Then Jim opened the protective case.

Inside lay a small grey clay tablet, its irregular surfaces crowded with impressions.

Susan examined it.

"Well preserved."

"Better than it looked in the ground," Jim said.

"Both faces?"

"And the edges."

"Good."

Mark placed it beneath the scanner. A magnified image rose above the transparent table.

He brought the reverse into view, reflected it and positioned the two surfaces for a full-field reading.

Nobody needed to explain what would happen next.

It was the purpose of the instrument.

Susan moved to her usual position beside the table. Jim stood opposite her with the field notes. Mark began the translation.

The images formed immediately.

A concentrated face appeared within the first few millimetres of movement, with animal forms attending it.

"Operator," Susan said.

Jim nodded.

"Two canine forms beside the ears."

"And another higher up," Mark added.

The reflected surface continued moving. The forms changed without leaving the field empty. One set of relationships became another; boundaries and expressions transformed as the figures shifted.

Susan followed the direction of the central figure's attention.

"He's listening."

"To the animals?" Jim asked.

"Receiving something from them, certainly."

Mark advanced the image.

The man's expression changed with the surrounding forms. Different animals occupied different positions, and the direction of attention changed accordingly.

Jim made a note.

"Relational sequence."

"Yes," Susan said. "Keep the whole field."

Mark was already recording it.

They worked as they always did, recognising figures, describing actions, comparing relationships. Susan occasionally asked him to slow the movement, not to search for an image, but to follow a gesture or an expression through its transformation.

The source was unusually small.

The imagery was exceptionally dense.

But neither of those facts required an explanation of the method.

It was an ancient document. They were reading it.

***

A younger figure appeared.

Susan leaned closer.

"Now there's a child."

Mark continued the translation.

The child occupied one relationship, then another. An older figure seemed to instruct her. As the field changed, the young person's attention followed something being presented.

"Education," Susan said.

Jim studied the arrangement.

"Teacher and student?"

"Look at the response. She's following the instruction."

The next configuration emerged.

And there she was again.

"Same child," Jim said.

Susan nodded.

Mark continued.

Another instructional scene took shape.

Susan watched the younger figure's posture, her attention, and the way the surrounding figures responded to her.

"Keep going, Mark."

He advanced a little farther.

The child remained the organising figure.

Another relationship developed around her. Another change in action.

Susan looked towards Jim.

"Have you read past this?"

"No. I brought you the object and the field record. The sequence is yours."

She returned to the image.

"Interesting."

Jim smiled.

"That's the reaction I was hoping for."

"Not the richness," Susan said. "Look at whom we're following."

Mark slowed the image.

"The child?"

"Yes."

The scene transformed again.

Susan recognised her immediately.

"Still her."

Jim leaned over the table.

"Another lesson."

"And another stage of the same person's development."

Mark continued.

Susan moved around to the opposite side. She was no longer concentrating only on the current scene. She was following the recurrence of the young person through successive changes in the field.

At the next configuration she smiled.

"There she is again."

Jim looked from the projection to the tiny piece of clay.

"That's unusual."

"Very."

He glanced at Susan.

"How unusual?"

She considered the question.

"We read records of communities, operations, technologies, conflicts, families. Individuals appear in those records, of course."

"But?"

Susan pointed towards the young figure.

"But this one keeps bringing us back to the same person."

Mark saved the continuing sequence.

Jim folded his arms.

"Are we following a biography?"

Susan watched the imagery change again.

"I think we may be."

He looked at the tablet.

"Three centimetres of clay."

Susan laughed softly.

"That doesn't seem to have discouraged its makers."

***

The three of them continued reading.

Mark kept the movement slow enough for them to examine the actions while preserving the continuous sequence. Jim recorded the repeating figures and changes in their relationships. Susan followed the young person's gestures and expressions.

The child was attentive, curious and responsive.

In one configuration she seemed to be watching an older figure demonstrate something. In another, the relationship changed, with the child responding through a different action.

Susan grew quieter as she watched.

Jim knew that silence.

It meant she had stopped thinking about the equipment, the room, and sometimes even the people beside her.

She was with the images.

Mark finally asked, "How much of the sequence do you want for today's record?"

"Everything we've passed through," Susan answered.

"Of course. I meant the detailed annotations."

Susan looked at him.

"All the relationships involving her. Especially where the actions change."

Mark nodded.

Jim made another note.

Then he glanced at the time.

"Susan, didn't you say you were meeting your mother?"

She looked towards the wall display.

"Oh, good heavens."

Mark smiled.

"You're late."

"Not yet."

"You will be by the time you reach the restaurant."

Susan looked back at the projection.

The young figure remained there, intent on the person before her.

"Just another minute."

Jim shook his head.

"No. Go and have lunch."

She regarded him.

"You've been in the field seven weeks. Don't tell me you're suddenly in a hurry."

"I'm not. That's why the tablet will still be here when you return."

Susan considered this.

"A fair point."

She gathered her things, then turned to Mark.

"Keep the image positions and the full movement together."

"They're already together."

"And don't run ahead."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

Jim gave her a look.

She gave him one back.

"Neither of you."

Jim raised both hands.

"Not a millimetre."

Susan smiled and headed for the door.

She stopped once.

Looked back towards the tiny object on the table.

"Jim?"

"Yes?"

"I'm very glad you brought this one home."

His face softened.

"So am I."

***

Outside, Veyra was settling into its afternoon.

Warm light moved through the elevated gardens. A train crossed the river below the pedestrian walkway, and boats travelled among the waterfront buildings. From a nearby terrace came the sound of people talking over lunch.

Susan passed a group of children emerging from their lessons.

Two were deep in conversation. Another had stopped to watch a bird working among the flowering branches. The adult accompanying them waited without hurrying the child.

Susan smiled.

She thought of the young figure on the tablet.

Not because children in ancient records were unusual.

Because the record seemed to keep returning to this particular one.

What had made her life worth preserving?

What had she learned?

And what would the later images show?

Her mother was already seated beneath a flowering tree overlooking the water.

She looked up as Susan approached.

"Sorry I'm late, Mum."

Her mother smiled.

"Something interesting at work?"

Susan pulled out a chair.

"You have no idea."

**END OF CHAPTER ONE**
