**Sean Diamond**

**Status:** Complete research paper / submission-ready research output.

## Abstract summary

This paper examines early human pattern recognition, including face-like pattern detection often labelled pareidolia, as an adaptive perceptual capacity rather than a defect to be dismissed by default. It connects infant face preference, filial-imprinting analogies, predictive processing, attachment, joint attention, epistemic trust, caregiver attunement, imaginative play, and visual meaning-making.

The paper argues that early meaningful-pattern detection should be studied as a real cognitive phenomenon whose developmental value depends partly on how it is scaffolded and tested. It also examines the possible effects of caregiver validation or repeated invalidation on exploratory confidence and self-trust.

## Relevance to Geonosis

This paper provides a cognitive foundation for Picterpretation by establishing an important distinction:

> The existence of human pattern-recognition capacity does not validate any particular archaeological reading.

Capacity is not correctness.

A person can be genuinely good at detecting structured visual relations and still make a false interpretation. Méthodê Glyph therefore treats perceptual capacity as the beginning of an audit, not the end of one.

## Methodological bridge

Within the wider research spine, this paper addresses the common objection that visual pattern recognition can be dismissed merely by applying the label “pareidolia.” The stronger scientific question is whether a proposed pattern survives controls:

- recurrence;
- constrained placement;
- independent detection;
- alternative-explanation testing;
- reproducibility;
- failure conditions;
- source and image integrity.

The project position is therefore neither “pareidolia proves the image” nor “pareidolia disproves the image.” It is that pattern perception is a human faculty whose outputs require disciplined testing.
